# Jeff Beaumont · Internal Writing Skill
### Version 1.1 — April 2026

## What internal writing is

Internal writing is produced for audiences with authority to act: leadership,
peers, direct reports. The voice is the same as public writing: direct, grounded,
human-first. The structure is more explicit and the sequencing more disciplined.

**Two hard rules that apply to every format:**
1. Always end with explicit next steps and named owners. If an action has no owner, it does not belong.
2. "We" over "I" wherever accurate. The work is a team effort.

---

## Em-dashes

Same rule as public writing. Target zero per document. Accept one or two only when
no good alternative exists. Default to periods, commas, colons, and semicolons.
Em-dashes are an AI writing signal. Flag every one in review.

## Format 1: Strategy and Reformulation Documents

Used for CS Ops strategy, Digital Strategy, Renewal Ops, VSM/VSA frameworks, and
any document diagnosing an area of the business and prescribing direction.

**Template sections in order:**

| Section | What goes here |
|---|---|
| **Charter** | One or two sentences. What does this function exist to do? Purpose, not tasks. |
| **Beliefs** | Short list of declarative sentences. Assumptions bridging diagnosis and action. Bold the principle name. One sentence each. |
| **Guiding Principles** | Bold name + one explanatory sentence. Answers "How do we operate?" not "What do we do?" |
| **Diagnosis** | Named problems, each as its own header. Specific problems, not general areas of concern. |
| **Coherent Set of Actions** | Explicitly labeled. Each action traces back to a specific diagnosis item. |
| **Success Criteria** | Observable outcomes. Measurable where possible. The test for whether the strategy worked. |

**Most important sequencing rule:** Diagnosis and actions belong in separate sections.
Never mix "what is wrong" with "what we are doing." The reader must be able to draw a
line from every action back to a specific diagnosis item.

**Belief sentence examples:**

Good: "Clarity is key; clarity of roles, expectations, mission, vision, and direction."
Bad: "We should work to ensure everyone has a clear understanding of their areas."

Good: "CS Ops must be proactive vs. reactive."
Bad: "We aim to be more strategic in our approach over time."

Good: "Every process improvement is ultimately a patient care decision."
Bad: "Our work connects to the broader mission of the organization."

## Format 2: Project Briefs and Proposals

Used for investment proposals, process changes, headcount requests, project approvals.
Goal: make the decision easy.

**SCR Template:**

| Section | What goes here |
|---|---|
| **Situation** | Current state, specific. Name the metric, process, gap. Do not assume shared context. |
| **Complication** | Why the current state is a problem. Name the cost in time, risk, money, or mission impact. This is where urgency lives. |
| **Resolution** | What to do and what it will deliver. Direct about the ask. State expected outcome. Anticipate the objection. |
| **Next Steps** | Who does what by when. Named owners. No action without a name attached. |

**Opening rules:**
- Open with the situation in one sentence. "Cash approval requests currently pass through four roles and average 2.3 days."
- State the ask explicitly and early.
- Include a mission link: trace the improvement back to the people it affects.

**Mission Link Statement:** One sentence tracing the benefit to the people affected.
Not inspirational language. The honest reason the work matters.

Example: "Reducing the cash approval cycle from 2.3 days to 1 day returns approximately
3 hours per week of crew administrative burden back to the wards."

## Format 3: Leadership Emails

- State the ask in the first sentence or subject line. Not buried. Not warmed up to.
- One email, one ask. Multiple unrelated asks = multiple emails or numbered items with explicit labels.
- Always end with next steps and named owners.
- Lead with conclusion, then provide context if needed. Context-before-conclusion buries the ask.
- "We" over "I" wherever accurate.

Good: "Asking for approval to proceed with [X]. Here is the situation and what we are recommending."
Bad: "Hope you are doing well. I wanted to reach out about something we have been working on..."

## Format 4: Weekly / Bi-Weekly Snapshot

The snapshot is a curated view of what matters, filtered through leadership's priorities.
It is not a status report.

**Template (follow exactly — plain text body, no markdown bold or headers in the email itself):**

```
Happy Friday.

Word count: [X] ([Y] minutes)

One big thing: [one sentence — names a tension, risk, or theme. Takes a position.]

Highlights
1. [Full sentence. Named people get credit.]
   * [sub-detail if needed]
2. [Full sentence.]

Insights
1. [What the data or events mean. Not more highlights.]
2. [Analysis. What leadership should pay attention to.]

Blockers / Watchpoint
1. (Blocker) [item — status and next step]
1. (Watchpoint) [item — what to watch and why]

Next
1. [Item for next week]
2. [Item for next week]

Thought for the day: [optional — dry, human, brief. Skip explicitly if the week is too serious.]
```

**Section formatting rules:**
- Headers are plain text labels, not bold or markdown-formatted
- Items are numbered (1. 2. 3.) not bulleted with dashes
- Sub-details use `*` on the next line, indented under the parent item
- ✅ inline with item name for completed items (used most in GLM Finance Update)
- No em-dashes in section headers or item labels

**Word count target:** 150-300 words. State word count and read time at the top.

**One Big Thing rules:**
- This is NOT the top highlight. It is the single sentence leadership most needs to understand this week.
- It names a tension, risk, or theme. It takes a position. It is not neutral.
- Strong examples:
  - Names a tension: "Month-end is clean, but the treasury chain is exposing a control gap we have not named yet."
  - Takes a position: "The FD bottleneck is not a process problem. It is a design problem."
  - Connects to mission: "Three hours returned to the wards this week because one approval workflow now takes one day instead of three."

**Section distinctions:**
- Highlights: What happened. Wins, progress, notable events. Named people get credit. Full sentences.
- Insights: What the data or events mean. Analysis. What leadership should pay attention to. Not more highlights.
- Blockers vs. Watchpoint: Blocker = actively preventing progress. Watchpoint = risk that may become a blocker. Label each explicitly with the prefix.

**Acknowledging misses:** State them directly and without qualification. "Will miss Q3 OKR because of Q3 GTP and other priorities." One sentence. Move on.

**Mercy Ships adaptations:**
- Shorter than GitLab-era snapshots: 130-254 words vs. 400+.
- "Blockers" becomes "Blockers/Watchpoint." Watchpoint is a softer category for risks not yet active.
- Mission presence in Highlights. Finance to Crew to Patients link, used once per snapshot at most, only when genuine.

**Thought for the day:** Dry, human, brief. Label it. Skip explicitly when the week is too serious: "Thought for the day: Skipping this week."

## I/we rule

- "I": personal perspective, argument, accountability for a decision.
- "we": what a team did together.
- Never "we" when taking a personal position. That diffuses accountability.
- Never "I" for something the team accomplished. That erases credit.

## Anti-patterns for internal writing

| Pattern | Why it fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Tactics disguised as strategy | Lists next steps with no diagnosis | Write diagnosis before actions |
| Executing on the proposed solution before diagnosing root problem | Addresses symptoms | Diagnosis section first |
| Actions with no named owners | Nothing will happen | Every action has a named owner and a date |
| Boiling the ocean | No decisions have been made | Name the two or three highest-leverage actions |
| Context before conclusion in emails | Buries the ask | Lead with conclusion |
| The "we"-less snapshot | Erases team effort | Use "we" for shared work |
| Blockers listed without status or next step | Creates alarm without resolution | Status, owner, timeline for each |
| Insights section that is actually just more highlights | Describes what happened, not what it means | Insights answers: what does this mean? What should change? |
| Em-dashes used to connect clauses | AI signal | Period or semicolon instead |

## What to give Claude by format

| Format | What AI needs |
|---|---|
| Strategy doc | Charter in one sentence, 3-5 diagnosis items (specific problems), guiding principles, 2-3 actions already confirmed |
| Proposal | Situation in one sentence, complication (what it costs and to whom), the ask, mission link |
| Leadership email | The ask, minimum needed context, any objection being preemptively addressed |
| Weekly snapshot | One Big Thing first, then highlights, insights, blockers. Word count target. Audience names and titles. |

## Pre-send checklist

- Every action has a named owner?
- Diagnosis in its own section, separate from actions?
- Every action traceable to a specific diagnosis item?
- Ask stated explicitly and early?
- Mission link included: one sentence tracing benefit to the people affected?
- "We" and "I" used correctly?
- For snapshots: is the One Big Thing actually the most important thing, not just the most recent highlight?
- For proposals: would a skeptical reader know what they are being asked to approve, and why it matters?
- Anything softened that should be stated directly?
- Em-dashes: 0-2 total? Any above that flagged and replaced?
